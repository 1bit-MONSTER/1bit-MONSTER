Qwen3.6-35B-A3B streams fully on the Ryzen™ AI NPU

NPU QWEN FLM · July 30, 2026 · bong-water-water-bong

AMD published official numbers for Qwen3.6-35B-A3B streaming fully on the Ryzen™ AI NPU: 13.65 tok/s decode at 1k context, 221.96 tok/s prefill at 32k, measured on a Ryzen AI 7 350 laptop with FastFlowLM v0.9.45. No GPU. No discrete accelerator. A 35B-parameter mixture-of-experts with 256 experts, running token by token on the NPU that ships inside the chip — and we extracted the exact same model's NPU builds from that stack two days ago. Here's what AMD's numbers actually say, and where our engine stands against them.

Context matters: FastFlowLM (FLM) is AMD's NPU-first runtime, now maintained inside AMD itself. The benchmark page is unambiguous — every row is labeled NPU (FLM). This is the closest thing the ecosystem has to an official "this model works on this NPU" stamp, and it's for a 35B MoE — the class of model that usually demands a discrete GPU or a big iGPU slice.

What AMD published

One laptop: AMD Ryzen™ AI 7 350 (Kraken Point), 96 GB DRAM, FLM v0.9.45, default NPU Performance power mode. Decode speed (tokens/second, per context length):

Correction: fastflowlm.com's note says the results are "based on FastFlowLM v0.9.45", but the published values match the v0.9.46 release notes exactly (v0.9.46: "New 13.65 vs Old 12.41" @1k decode = the site's numbers). Treat the site table as v0.9.46 results. We ran the same v0.9.46 build — see our measured table below.

Read the shape of the curves, not just the peak. Decode drops gently from 13.65 to 9.51 tok/s as context grows 1k → 32k — that's the KV-cache feeding cost, and a 30% hit at 32k is good for an NPU. Prefill does the opposite: it scales up with prompt length, hitting 221.96 tok/s at 32k. NPUs are embarrassingly parallel machines; long prompts are exactly where they shine.

A 35B-parameter MoE with 256 experts, streaming token-by-token, entirely on the NPU inside a laptop SoC. That's the headline. 13 tok/s is the footnote.

Why "fully on NPU" is the hard part

Most "NPU" demos are hybrid: attention or some layers offloaded to the GPU, the NPU doing one big GEMM. Qwen3.6-35B-A3B is different — it's a 256-expert MoE with 3B active parameters out of 35B total. Every token has to route through a router, activate a subset of 256 experts, and gather their outputs. On an NPU that means expert weights must be resident, addressable, and dispatchable on-device — no help from the iGPU. AMD's own homepage claims their runtime is "the only NPU-first runtime built for AMD Ryzen AI" and holds the whole context window on-device.

We know this stack from the inside. We reverse-engineered FLM's proprietary NPU plumbing in four days (22 proprietary .so → 17.5 MB open source) and extracted the pre-built model zoo from the ROCm/FastFlowLM v0.9.46 mirror: 37 models, 209 compiled xclbins, including Qwen3.6-MoE-35B — 256 experts, 3B active, 40 layers, 262k context, 9 NPU xclbin stanzas with Peano-compiled INT8 kernels for QKV, O, and the expert gates.

So when AMD says "fully on NPU," we can read the binary evidence behind it: the expert router weights, the gate projections, and all 256 expert tensors are compiled into NPU programs, not GPU shaders.

A GPU decodes 2–4× faster. That's physics: the NPU is a throughput engine, not a latency engine, and MoE routing on a 6×8 XDNA 2 array has real dispatch overhead. But tok/s isn't the only axis — AMD's own power tests on the same chip claim 67.2× less energy per token than the integrated GPU and 222.9× less than the CPU, at comparable or higher throughput. For a long-running local agent, that is the difference between a warm laptop and a jet engine.

NPU inference isn't about beating llama.cpp's tok/s. It's about getting the same tokens for 67× less energy, on a chip that's otherwise idle.

Verified against the official sources

We checked the claim against the model's official release before writing any of this. Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (Hugging Face, 2026-04-16, Apache-2.0, 6.1M downloads) is real, and its official config.json confirms: 35.95B params, 256 experts (8 active/token), 40 layers (30 GatedDeltaNet linear-attention + 10 full-attention, interval 4), 262k context — and it's a vision-language model (image-text-to-text), not text-only.

One more fact that matters for anyone running this locally: we diffed the official configs of Qwen3.5-35B-A3B and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B field-by-field and they are byte-identical — same qwen3_5_moe architecture, same dims, same vocab, same expert count. Qwen3.6 is a newer checkpoint of the exact architecture we already run end-to-end at 20 tok/s. And worth noting: FastFlowLM appears in none of Qwen's official framework lists (transformers, llama.cpp, MLX, SGLang, vLLM) — AMD's NPU support is their own stack, which is exactly the stack we reverse-engineered.

Qwen3.6-35B-A3B is a new checkpoint of an architecture we already run: identical config, verified against the official model files.

Where our engine stands

Our NPU path went through the same stack AMD ships, then got rebuilt natively. On 2026-07-28 we replaced FLM's 22 proprietary .so with our own npu_engine_universal — INT8 GEMM ops verified 22/22 shapes across 5 models with 0/10000 errors on real XDNA 2 hardware, plus raw device-level validation at 51 TOPS INT8 GEMM. The old FLM-based fused engine (v12, 97 tok/s M=32 batch) is superseded; the xclbins we extracted are the same binaries AMD compiles with Peano.

This morning we put the first measured number on the board for the Qwen3.6 checkpoint itself: 75.65 tok/s decode (flat at 75.95 tok/s @8k context) and 1105.71 tok/s prefill via llama.cpp Vulkan on our Strix Halo box, Q4_K_M, all 99 layers offloaded — 5.5× AMD's official NPU decode, 5–14× their prefill. Full log: benchmarks/RESULTS-qwen3.6-35b-a3b-vulkan-2026-07-30.md.

Then, last night, we closed the loop: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B now runs fully on the NPU in this repo's benchmark harness — same software AMD publishes with (FastFlowLM v0.9.46, the version whose numbers are on fastflowlm.com), official Q4_K_S weights from FastFlowLM/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-NPU2 on Hugging Face, and the 9 compiled xclbins. Same software version (v0.9.46), different hardware (Strix Halo MAX+ 395 vs Kraken Point Ryzen AI 7 350). Prefill beats AMD's published numbers at every context length (+8% to +24%); decode trails by 7–15%. Honest read: Kraken Point is FLM's reference laptop platform — the decode gap is likely platform-tuned kernels, not a software gap.

What's still open is the run we actually care about: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B end-to-end on our native open-source engine — npu_engine_universal with the 9 extracted xclbins. That engine has no MoE expert routing yet (ModelConfig has no expert fields), so the FLM stack runs this model while our own stack still needs the MoE decode loop, shared expert, and GateDeltaNet attention paths. The architecture is already proven at 20 tok/s e2e on the 3.5 checkpoint, and the 1BP conversion is built. That's the item at the top of our queue — and the 11.66 tok/s we measured last night is the number to beat.

What this means

  • A 35B MoE on an NPU is real, official, and shipping. AMD stamped it; the binaries are in the wild; we extracted them.
  • Long context is the NPU's home turf. 221.96 tok/s prefill at 32k means ingesting huge documents on-device, then decoding at 9–13 tok/s — a fine trade for a battery-powered agent.
  • The remaining gap is runtime engineering, not hardware. Same xclbins, same NPU, open driver. The model runs; the question is how well our scheduler feeds it.
